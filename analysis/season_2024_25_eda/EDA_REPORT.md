# 2024–25 season EDA report

All numbers come from the CSVs in [data/](data/), regenerated by [analyze.py](analyze.py). See [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) for scope and denominator rules.

## 1. The season

The 2024–25 ACF season is the only complete season in the corpus. Four question-set events, 33 mirror sites, 2,661 games, 64,037 tossup attempts, 49,042 bonuses heard.

| Tier | Date | Sites | Teams | Games | Player IDs | Attempts | Bonuses heard |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall | 2024-10-19 | 8 | 141 | 744 | 542 | 17,682 | 14,112 |
| Winter | 2024-11-16 | 10 | 166 | 758 | 674 | 18,136 | 14,104 |
| Regionals | 2025-02-01 | 14 | 137 | 747 | 504 | 18,050 | 13,232 |
| Nationals | 2025-04-19 | 1 | 48 | 412 | 182 | 10,169 | 7,594 |

Attempt volume is remarkably flat across the first three tiers (17.7k–18.1k) even though site counts run 8 → 10 → 14, because 2025 Regionals sites were smaller on average. Nationals is a single invitational site.

Tier is chronological here, so every figure that reads left-to-right is also reading forward in time. It is simultaneously the difficulty axis, which is convenient but means the two cannot be separated within this scope.

## 2. Difficulty is a timing gradient, not an accuracy gradient

This is the central finding of the season view.

| Tier | All-attempt accuracy | **Scored accuracy** | Neg rate | Zero rate | Median position | PPB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall | 0.799 | **0.829** | 0.165 | 0.037 | 66.7% | 17.96 |
| Winter | 0.778 | **0.822** | 0.169 | 0.053 | 72.4% | 15.36 |
| Regionals | 0.734 | **0.818** | 0.164 | 0.103 | 81.6% | 14.18 |
| Nationals | 0.747 | **0.811** | 0.175 | 0.079 | 80.4% | 15.26 |

All-attempt accuracy falls 6.5 points across the season. **Scored accuracy falls 1.9 points.** Neg rate does not move at all — it sits in a 1.1-point band.

What actually responds to set difficulty:

- **When people buzz.** Median position moves 14 points later from Fall to Regionals.
- **How often nobody converts.** Zero rate nearly triples, 3.7% → 10.3%.
- **Bonus conversion.** PPB falls 3.8 points from Fall to Regionals.

Conditional on someone taking a scored buzz, they are right about 82% of the time no matter which set they are playing. That is a behavioural equilibrium, not a coincidence: on a harder set players wait longer before buzzing, which costs them tossups that die unanswered but holds their conversion rate roughly constant.

**Why this matters for the models in this repository.** The difficulty signal lives in buzz timing and dead-tossup rate, not in conditional correctness. That is precisely what the Stage-1 competing-risks survival likelihood is built around — a hazard over token positions plus a dead-tossup survival term. A model that only predicted "given a buzz, was it right" would see almost no difficulty variation across this season. It also means the zero/dead-tossup rows are carrying real signal, not noise to be discarded.

Nationals partly breaks the monotone pattern — it is easier than Regionals on all-attempt accuracy (0.747 vs 0.734), PPB (15.26 vs 14.18), and zero rate (7.9% vs 10.3%), and only slightly harder on scored accuracy. Some of that is a stronger field rather than an easier set, and these descriptive statistics cannot separate the two. Section 5 gets closer by holding the set fixed.

## 3. Players

1,339 player IDs have at least one buzz; median 33 attempts; 718 clear 30 attempts, the threshold for every player-level statistic below.

The player-level accuracy/timing relationship reproduces the artifact exactly: mean buzz position correlates **−0.314** with all-attempt accuracy and **+0.036** with scored accuracy. Once value-0 attempts are out of the denominator, a player's average buzz timing says essentially nothing about whether they convert.

## 4. The season funnels, but the field is not a cohort

Player overlap between tiers, as a share of the row tier's players:

| Of players who played… | …also played Fall | Winter | Regionals | Nationals |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Fall** | 100% | 25% | 25% | 3% |
| **Winter** | 20% | 100% | 32% | 11% |
| **Regionals** | 27% | 42% | 100% | 23% |
| **Nationals** | 10% | 40% | 64% | 100% |

Read forward, retention is low: only 3% of Fall players reach Nationals. Read backward, continuity is high: 64% of Nationals players played Regionals and 40% played Winter.

898 of 1,339 players appear at exactly one tier, 326 at two, 108 at three, and **7 at all four**.

Carry-over nevertheless accumulates steadily through the season:

| Tier | Player IDs | Seen earlier | Share of IDs | Share of attempts |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall | 542 | 0 | — | — |
| Winter | 674 | 136 | 20.2% | 18.1% |
| Regionals | 504 | 295 | 58.5% | 56.0% |
| Nationals | 182 | 132 | 72.5% | 72.1% |

So the field is neither a stable cohort nor a fresh draw each time. Fall and Winter are largely separate populations (only 20–25% overlap), while Regionals and Nationals are heavily drawn from players already observed.

For any model that shares player parameters across the season, this means the amount of pooled evidence per player varies enormously by tier — most Fall players are seen once and never again, while most Nationals players arrive with two or three prior events of history.

## 5. Selection: what actually distinguishes players who advance

Comparing player performance across tiers is confounded, because the sets differ. Fixing the set removes that. For each of Fall, Winter, and Regionals, take the players with at least 20 attempts *on that set* and group them by how far they later go in the season:

| Fixed set | Furthest tier | Players | Scored accuracy | Mean position |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall | Fall | 170 | 0.811 | 68.1% |
| Fall | Winter | 64 | 0.840 | 66.4% |
| Fall | Regionals | 105 | 0.831 | 65.1% |
| Fall | Nationals | 17 | 0.871 | 60.0% |
| Winter | Winter | 185 | 0.813 | 75.1% |
| Winter | Regionals | 116 | 0.811 | 70.5% |
| Winter | Nationals | 69 | 0.852 | 62.4% |
| Regionals | Regionals | 255 | 0.797 | 80.1% |
| Regionals | Nationals | 105 | 0.842 | 70.0% |

Two things stand out.

**Timing separates players far more cleanly than accuracy does.** On every fixed set, mean buzz position is strictly monotone in how far a player goes, and the gaps are large: 8.1 points of the question on the Fall set, 12.7 on the Winter set, 10.1 on the Regionals set. Scored accuracy also increases but by less — 6.0, 3.9, and 4.5 points — and it is not monotone on the Fall set, where the Winter-furthest group (0.840) edges out the Regionals-furthest group (0.831).

**The effect is real but should not be overstated.** The accuracy differences are a handful of percentage points on a base near 0.81, and the Fall-set Nationals cell rests on 17 players. The timing result is the robust one.

This is again the same structural message as section 2: on a fixed set, the thing that distinguishes a stronger player is *how early they can buzz*, not how reliably they convert once they do. A latent-skill model that shifts the buzz hazard is capturing the right quantity; one that only models correctness given a buzz is not.

## 6. Teams and games

Restricting to the 480 team entries with at least 5 games, 30 attempts, and 15 bonuses heard:

- Scored accuracy correlates **+0.269** with PPB. All-attempt accuracy correlates +0.694, but that is inflated because the zero rate drives both.
- **Zero rate correlates −0.774 with PPB** — the strongest single relationship among team metrics, and another sign that "how often your buzzes die" is the dominant strength signal.
- Neg rate correlates **−0.070** with PPB. Aggressive teams are not worse at bonuses.

| Tier | Mean team score | Median absolute margin |
|---|---:|---:|
| Fall | 255.5 | 145 |
| Winter | 225.9 | 147.5 |
| Regionals | 204.3 | 160 |
| Nationals | 222.0 | 95 |

Scores fall as the season gets harder, then recover at Nationals. Margins widen through Regionals — an open-entry regional draws the most heterogeneous field — then collapse to 95 at Nationals, by far the most competitive event.

## 7. Questions

- Mean buzz position correlates **−0.488** with per-question conversion.
- Tossup length correlates **+0.354** with mean *normalized* position: longer questions are answered later even as a fraction of themselves.
- Clue-span count correlates **−0.006** with conversion — nothing at all.
- Bonus PPB correlates **+0.756** with 30-point rate.

Bonus-part conversion by declared difficulty:

| Tier | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall | 0.945 | 0.607 | 0.244 |
| Winter | 0.915 | 0.473 | 0.148 |
| Regionals | 0.891 | 0.412 | 0.115 |
| Nationals | 0.942 | 0.489 | 0.094 |

Easy > medium > hard everywhere, and the spread widens with tier difficulty. The set is written to spread a strong field at the top of the bonus, not to punish it at the bottom.

## 9. Diagnostics that point to CompIRT adjustments

The descriptive results above say that timing matters. The following diagnostics get closer to *how* the Stage-1 model should represent it.

### 9.1 Buzz hazard has clue-local structure

Among the 51,037 scored phase-0 first buzzes used by the baseline Stage-1 event construction, **17.05% occur exactly on a dataset-provided sentence/clue boundary**. Only 4.67% of the available token positions in those same questions are boundaries, so exact-boundary buzzing is enriched **3.65×** over uniform token opportunity. The pattern is not driven by only one mark: over all recorded attempts, the enrichment is 3.81× for correct buzzes and 3.62× for negs.

The broader terminal tenth of a clue span holds 30.2% of phase-0 first buzzes against 13.3% of token opportunities, an enrichment of 2.27×. This is not merely the season-wide tendency to buzz late in a tossup: each position is measured relative to the start and end of its *current clue*.

**Model implication.** A smooth function of normalized tossup position misses a sharp, repeated within-clue feature of the hazard. Clue-aligned difficulty curves are therefore structurally well motivated. A smaller adjustment would add clue phase or a boundary/pause indicator to the hazard branch. The uniform-token comparison is an opportunity baseline, not a fitted survival null, so the choice should still be judged by held-out event likelihood and first-buzz calibration. Boundary heaping may combine actual listening behaviour with how buzz positions were recorded.

### 9.2 Returning-player parameters are stable enough to carry forward

For every scored attempt, subtract that tossup's mean timing and scored accuracy. Then average these question-centered residuals for each player and compare returning players across adjacent tiers, requiring at least 20 scored attempts in each tier:

| Transition | Players | Timing residual r | Correctness residual r |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fall → Winter | 69 | 0.540 | 0.499 |
| Winter → Regionals | 141 | 0.716 | 0.423 |
| Regionals → Nationals | 101 | 0.736 | 0.545 |

Both latent signals persist despite entirely different questions, and timing is especially stable in the later transitions. This supports the returning-player posterior priors used by the holdout runners. It does **not** support freezing a player's parameter indefinitely: correlations are well below one, the Fall/Winter population turns over heavily, and the gaps between events are long enough for real drift. A dynamic prior centred on the previous posterior with an estimable transition variance is the natural extension.

### 9.3 Category specialization repeats for timing, not correctness

Split the season into Fall+Winter and Regionals+Nationals. Within each half, subtract each question's mean and then each player's general residual, leaving a player×category specialty. Among the 239 player-category cells with at least 10 scored attempts in that category in *both* halves:

- timing specialty repeats at **r = 0.387**;
- correctness specialty repeats at **r = 0.009**.

The contrast is stable across thresholds:

| Minimum scored attempts per category-half | Cells | Timing specialty r | Correctness specialty r |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 554 | 0.331 | −0.011 |
| 8 | 328 | 0.366 | −0.035 |
| 10 | 239 | 0.387 | 0.009 |
| 15 | 122 | 0.484 | −0.010 |

**Model implication.** Category-aware or multidimensional skill has a reproducible target in the hazard/timing branch. The same data provide no descriptive support for freely estimated category-specific correctness skill at these sample sizes. In a double-difficulty model, hazard relevance weights can plausibly be richer while correctness dimensions should be strongly shrunk, lower-rank, or selected only when they improve held-out calibration.

### 9.4 Hazard and correctness should not be forced onto one curve

Across the 1,360 questions, mean **scored-buzz** position correlates only −0.279 with scored accuracy. Within individual tiers the correlation ranges from −0.133 to −0.411. No-buzz rate and scored accuracy correlate −0.399 pooled. These signals are related, but one explains only a modest share of the other's question-level variation.

That is direct descriptive support for the double-difficulty families in this repository: use separate question curves/relevance weights for whether someone buzzes and whether that buzz is correct. A shared latent player representation may still be useful—the stability results above show that it is—but forcing the two observation branches to share all item-time structure is unnecessarily restrictive.

### Adjustment priority suggested by this EDA

1. Preserve the competing-risks likelihood and terminal survival rows.
2. Prefer clue-aware hazard curves; test a boundary or clue-phase term if splines remain too smooth.
3. Carry returning-player posteriors forward with drift rather than using either a fresh standard prior or a frozen estimate.
4. Allow category/multidimensional structure primarily in the hazard branch and shrink correctness dimensions harder.
5. Keep hazard and correctness item curves separate, and choose added flexibility by held-out joint event likelihood/calibration.

These are hypotheses for model comparison, not claims that the most flexible version must win.

## 10. What this analysis does not do

It is descriptive. No IRT model is fitted, no latent skill estimated, no held-out prediction made. Every difficulty number is a raw rate confounded with who was in the room, and within a single season tier is confounded with calendar time — an event being "harder" and being "later in the season" cannot be separated here.

Correlations are Pearson on the stated subsets, with no significance tests, confidence intervals, or multiple-comparison corrections. The selection analysis in section 5 conditions on an outcome (how far a player later goes) and is therefore descriptive of selection, not causal about development. The clue-position comparison uses uniform token opportunity as a transparent reference, not as a realistic constant-hazard null. The parameter-stability and category-specialty calculations residualize raw outcomes; they are diagnostics, not latent-variable estimates.
